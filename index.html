Cluster I  ·  5 Businesses

Building the AI Substrate

Five businesses physically constructing the chips, foundries, racks, software, and cloud capacity the AI economy runs on top of. The activity is build. The megatrend is a multi-trillion-dollar buildout of compute infrastructure unfolding across this decade.

NVIDIA NVDA

MegatrendThe build-out of AI factories. These are giant, power-hungry data centers whose only product is intelligence.

Every kind of AI buyer now runs more of its AI on NVIDIA. That includes the largest cloud companies, the leading AI labs, governments, and the makers of self-driving cars and robots. Q2 sales reached $96 billion, more than double a year ago. Growth sped up for the fourth straight quarter. The one soft spot: memory costs spiked, so the company cut its profit-margin outlook into next year.

  • Q2 revenue reached $96 billion, more than double a year earlier. Data-center sales rose 18% from the prior quarter to $89 billion. The company expects $108 billion in Q3.
  • For the first time, NVIDIA forecast a full year ahead: revenue up about 70% next year. Customer forecasts point to revenue doubling instead. Supply sets the limit.
  • Vera Rubin, the next AI system, began production shipments in August 2026. Every major hyperscaler (giant cloud company) has already ordered it. Amazon’s cloud (AWS) will add another 2 million NVIDIA chips, starting in Q3.
  • NVIDIA can earn more from every data center it equips. Per gigawatt (a unit of power that sizes a data center), its potential revenue rose from $18 billion with older Hopper chips to $25 billion with today’s Blackwell. With Vera Rubin it is $40 billion.
  • Gross margin held at 75% in Q2, but rising memory prices will squeeze it. Memory now costs more than NVIDIA expected, and prices are still climbing. Margin should slip to about 74% in Q3 and bottom at 71% to 72% in Q4. It recovers to 72% to 73% next year, once NVIDIA’s own price increases take effect.

The fastest growth again came from outside the giant clouds. Sales to AI start-ups, specialist AI clouds, enterprises, and governments reached $40 billion, up 138% in a year. This means the buyers who will never design their own chips are the ones growing fastest. They need a whole factory, not a part. And each generation captures more of that factory, so NVIDIA grows faster than the number of data centers. The margin dip comes from the same boom. Memory is scarce because AI buyers want so much of it.

The bottom line

We own NVIDIA, so we sit underneath every layer of a multi-trillion-dollar AI buildout. The customer base keeps widening. And the chips already out in the world keep gaining rental value.

Advanced Micro Devices AMD

MegatrendThe same AI infrastructure boom. But here it is a two-horse race, and the world’s hyperscalers (the giant cloud companies) want to keep it that way.

Demand for AI chips has outrun what NVIDIA alone can supply. So every cloud company, server maker, and AI lab now orders from AMD as the credible second source. Q2 added Anthropic as a third gigawatt-scale buyer, and revenue grew 50% to a record $11.5 billion.

  • Q2 revenue grew 50% year-over-year to $11.5 billion. Data-center revenue more than doubled, up 107% to a record $6.7 billion, and is now 58% of sales
  • Gross margin widened to 56%, up more than two points in a year. Earnings per share rose about 82%
  • Anthropic will deploy up to two gigawatts of AMD’s MI450 AI chips, starting in the first half of 2027. Microsoft will also deploy AMD’s AI racks at scale on Azure, its cloud
  • Helios, AMD’s pre-built AI server rack, is in production. First shipments begin in Q3 and ramp into 2027, with demand tracking ahead of AMD’s initial forecast
  • AMD expects Q3 revenue of about $13 billion, up 41%. It expects server-processor revenue (the standard CPUs every server runs on) to grow more than 80% year-over-year in the second half. Data-center revenue should more than double in 2027

Underneath the AI-chip headlines, the server-processor business keeps compounding. It posted its fifth straight record quarter, with cloud and enterprise sales each up more than 70%. Both units and prices grew by double digits, and units grew faster. This means AMD is selling more chips to more buyers, not just charging more for each one. And the next wave comes from AI agents (software that runs multi-step tasks on its own), which run on ordinary CPUs. AMD now sizes the server-processor market at about $220 billion by 2030. Agent servers are its smallest piece today and its largest by then. Supply ran tight this year because much of this demand arrived unforecast. Next year it is planned for, so supply should limit growth less.

The bottom line

AMD is the kind of business we want to own. It compounds in two markets on different schedules: a permanent second seat in AI chips, and the standard server CPU market every server already runs on.

Taiwan Semiconductor TSM

MegatrendThe world cannot build advanced AI without one factory network in Taiwan. It is the chosen manufacturer for almost every leading-edge chip on earth.

Every designer of the most advanced chips on earth (NVIDIA, AMD, Apple, Broadcom, Qualcomm) sends its designs to TSMC. No other factory can match TSMC’s manufacturing process. In Q2, revenue reached $40.2 billion, at the top of its own forecast, and margins widened. TSMC raised its full-year forecast to growth slightly above 40%, and raised its spending budget to match.

  • Q2 revenue reached $40.2 billion. Gross margin rose 1.5 points to 67.7%, helped by efficiency gains and fuller factories
  • High-performance computing (the data-center chips that run AI) grew 20% from the prior quarter, to 66% of sales. Smartphone chips fell 4% from the prior quarter
  • TSMC expects Q3 revenue of $44.6–45.8 billion, up 37% from a year ago at the midpoint. Gross margin should dip to about 66% as the new 2-nanometer process ramps
  • TSMC lifted its 2026 capital spending (money for new factories and tools) to $60–64 billion. It also committed another $100 billion to Arizona, roughly four more factories
  • Consumer and price-sensitive markets are weakening as component prices rise. AI demand remains extremely strong

Underneath the headline, TSMC is spending hard to narrow a supply gap that remains very big. The budget has climbed from $52–56 billion in January to $60–64 billion now. Part of that rise is simply that tools cost more. The next three years of spending will run even further above the last three. This means the coming margin dip is a startup cost, not weaker pricing. A new process runs less efficiently while it ramps. TSMC expects the 2-nanometer ramp to trim gross margin by 3 to 4 points in the second half. The cost fades as the new factories fill, and with demand far ahead of supply, they will fill fast.

The bottom line

TSMC is the rare business with no second supplier at the leading edge. Customers commit years in advance. Governments help fund the factories. Manufacturing prices keep rising. We get to own a piece of it.

CoreWeave CRWV

MegatrendRenting AI supercomputers by the hour. A new kind of cloud company exists solely to host the most demanding AI workloads on earth.

The largest AI labs and cloud companies are signing longer contracts to rent CoreWeave’s AI computing capacity. In Q2, revenue rose 112% from a year earlier, to $2.6 billion. Adjusted operating profit (profit from operations, before stock pay and one-time items) grew sixfold from Q1. The company raised its full-year outlook.

  • Q2 revenue reached $2.6 billion, up 112% from a year earlier and 24% from Q1. Adjusted operating margin rose to 5%, from about 1% in Q1, as profit jumped to $128 million from $21 million.
  • Contracted future revenue (signed deals not yet delivered) reached $104.2 billion, up 246% from a year earlier. That total excludes more than $25 billion of new commitments signed in the first weeks of Q3
  • CoreWeave added nearly 500 megawatts of running power in Q2, the most it has ever added in a quarter, bringing the total to 1.5 gigawatts. Contracted power has since grown to 4.2 gigawatts
  • The company raised its 2026 revenue outlook to $12.4–13.2 billion. It now expects to exit the year at an annual revenue pace of $18.5–19.5 billion
  • Its new managed inference service (running customers’ finished AI models on demand) grew booked annual revenue from $1 million to more than $100 million within months of launch

Underneath the growth, CoreWeave’s margins have started to rise, and scale is the main reason. A new build costs money before it earns. Each new data center now joins a far larger base of capacity that already earns, so its start-up cost weighs less on margins. Old hardware stays in that earning base: CoreWeave just signed a contract running into 2029 for A100 chips, a 2020 design. Richer contracts add to the gain. Deals signed in Q2 carry contribution margins (revenue minus direct costs) 5 to 10 percentage points above those of deals from recent quarters. CoreWeave also raised prices about 25% across chip types in July, after the quarter closed.

The bottom line

CoreWeave gives us a share in the specialized AI computing capacity the largest AI labs and cloud companies depend on. NVIDIA backs it. Customers commit years of revenue in advance, and the data centers are already running today.

Oracle ORCL

MegatrendA 50-year-old database company is reinventing itself as the cloud landlord of choice for AI. It serves customers who want their existing business data to be the fuel for AI.

AI labs train their models on Oracle. Large enterprises replace SAP and Workday with Oracle Fusion. Existing database customers now run Oracle’s database and its new AI data platform inside whichever cloud they already use. All three businesses grew in Q2. Revenue rose 30% to $19.3 billion, and profit per share rose 30%. The company raised its full-year revenue outlook to at least $90 billion.

  • Cloud infrastructure revenue reached $7.4 billion, up 121% year over year. Growth sped up from 93% the quarter before
  • Oracle delivered 850 megawatts of AI capacity with more than 300,000 GPUs (the chips that run AI). That delivery is almost 3x the prior quarter’s, and 73% of everything Oracle delivered in all of the prior fiscal year
  • Customers signed more than $30 billion of new AI contracts that require no new capital spending by Oracle
  • Revenue from Multicloud (Oracle’s database running inside AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud) grew 353% year over year. The number of Multicloud customers grew 180%, and the footprint now spans 70 regions
  • Fusion (Oracle’s enterprise software) grew 14%, and industry applications grew more than 20%. Inside these applications, customers now run more than 2,300 AI agents, up 90% in one quarter

Underneath the growth, the oldest chips are gaining value. Capacity that came up for renewal in Q2 was renewed or resold at prices 20% above the old contracts. Most of those chips were four years old or more. So Oracle’s oldest chips earn more as they age, not less.

The bottom line

Owning Oracle gives us a piece of three businesses at once. The first is AI infrastructure for the labs. The second is Multicloud, which delivers Oracle (database plus new AI data platform) to existing customers wherever they already run. The third is Fusion software, which replaces legacy systems across major enterprises. The AI infrastructure business alone has built a contracted future revenue base larger than any individual cloud giant’s.

Cluster II  ·  6 Businesses

Putting AI to Work Inside the Enterprise

Six businesses weaving AI into how large organizations actually operate: analyzing their data, watching their software run, designing their products, building their applications. The activity is operationalize. The megatrend is the migration of AI from demo to deployment inside the institutions that run the economy.

Palantir PLTR

MegatrendBig institutions race to embed AI in their core operations. They run it on their own messy data, in production.

The largest institutions on earth run more of their critical operations on Palantir’s software. They span defense, manufacturing, healthcare, banking, energy, and government. In Q2, revenue grew 93%, Palantir’s fastest growth as a public company. The company also raised its full-year outlook by more than ever before.

  • Q2 revenue grew 93% year over year to $1.935 billion. Expenses grew 37%, far slower than revenue, so the business became more efficient. Adjusted operating margin (operating profit before stock pay, as a share of revenue) was 62%. Free cash flow (cash left after running the business) was $1.22 billion, 63% of revenue.
  • U.S. commercial revenue grew 149% year over year to $764 million. U.S. businesses signed contracts worth $2.132 billion in total, nearly $800 million above the prior record quarter.
  • Existing customers spent 57% more than a year earlier. That is net dollar retention of 157%, up 7 percentage points from Q1.
  • U.S. government revenue grew 90% year over year to $809 million. For the first time, a program of record (a standing, budgeted military program) chose to run on Palantir’s Maven platform.
  • Full-year revenue guidance rose to a midpoint of $8.154 billion, which would be 82% growth.

Underneath the headline, trials are turning into long contracts within months. A nonprofit health system ran a pilot at the end of 2025; in Q2 it signed a three-year, $37 million partnership. Customers commit for years because what they build keeps its value whichever AI model wins. What they build lives in the Ontology (Palantir’s layer that lets AI act on a company’s own data). It holds a customer’s data, rules, and actions, and lets the customer switch models or train its own.

The bottom line

Palantir is the operating layer that lets the largest institutions on earth run AI on their own messy, sensitive data. Those institutions include defense agencies, factories, hospitals, banks, and governments. Every one of them wants this capability. The demand is among the largest in software. We are fortunate to own a piece of it.

Snowflake SNOW

MegatrendCompanies want one trusted, governed home for all of their data so AI agents can act on it.

Large enterprises run more work through Snowflake. They run analytics queries there, and now AI agents act on the same data. In Q2, revenue growth accelerated again, to 37%. It was the third straight quarter of faster growth.

  • Q2 product revenue grew 37% to $1.49 billion. Growth was 30% two quarters earlier. The dollar gain over Q1 set a company record for the second straight quarter.
  • Adjusted operating margin rose more than 4 points from a year ago, to 15%. Slower headcount growth helped. Year to date, Snowflake added 334 employees, 173 through an acquisition, versus 935 in the same period last year.
  • Cortex Code, or CoCo (Snowflake’s AI coding assistant), passed 9,100 accounts, adding more than 2,000 in Q2 alone. CoWork, which lets business users work with company data in plain English, reached 5,800 accounts, up nearly 11% from Q1.
  • AI products drove about half of the acceleration. Faster moves off older systems and other newer tools drove the rest. One equipment maker will finish moving off its old system in under three quarters, a job that once took years.
  • Snowflake added 692 net-new customers in Q2, up 32% from a year ago. Sixty-five customers now spend more than $10 million a year. Signed contracts not yet counted as revenue grew 30% to $9 billion.

Underneath the headline, AI pulls customers deeper into the core platform. Accounts that adopt CoCo run more of their core work on Snowflake. AI also helps new customers reach 80% of their purchased usage faster than earlier groups did. So AI is not a side business.

Snowflake keeps less of each dollar from AI work today, so it trimmed its gross margin outlook to 74%. But it runs its sales, finance and marketing on its own AI tools, so it needs fewer new hires to grow. That efficiency gain is why operating margin widens even as the AI mix trims gross margin.

The bottom line

Snowflake is the rare business we get to own that earns more every time a customer adds more data to the platform or runs more AI on top of it. New customers keep coming. AI work per customer keeps growing. And the leading AI labs run their models directly inside Snowflake.

Datadog DDOG

MegatrendSoftware keeps getting more complex. Someone has to watch it run. That matters even more now that AI agents write and ship the code.

Engineering teams, security teams, and AI coding agents all use Datadog more. In Q2, revenue grew 36% and growth sped up again. But its largest customer, an AI company, is cutting back, so Datadog expects Q3 growth to ease to 28% to 29%.

  • Q2 revenue rose 36% to $1.12 billion, above the top of Datadog’s own forecast. The $115 million added since Q1 was a record by a wide margin.
  • Two neolabs (AI labs that did not exist a few years ago) signed seven-figure annual deals to watch their model-training runs. One of the world’s largest online media companies signed a multiyear deal worth over $30 million, replacing four other tools.
  • More than 750 AI companies use Datadog, including all of the top 10. Of the 750, 31 pay more than $1 million a year and 8 pay more than $10 million.
  • Datadog grew more efficient, even as gross margin dipped to 79.6% from 80.9% a year ago. Operating expenses grew 26%, well below revenue’s 36%. So operating margin (the share of revenue left after running costs) rose to 23% from 20% a year ago.
  • The largest customer renewed with a nine-figure contract across 17 products. But its usage falls starting in Q3. Even so, Datadog expects full-year revenue of $4.45 to $4.47 billion, up 30%.

Underneath the headline, the rest of the business keeps speeding up. Revenue from non-AI customers grew in the high 20s percent, up from the mid-20s last quarter and 18% a year ago. So the softer Q3 forecast comes from the largest customer’s cutback, not a tiring business.

The bottom line

Datadog earns more every time more software runs in production, and we get to share in that flywheel. AI speeds up the pace of new software. AI-native companies consolidate their tooling onto Datadog. And AI coding agents themselves now call Datadog for production data.

Figma FIG

MegatrendAI makes building software cheap. So design becomes the thing that sets one product apart from another. The value moves to taste, judgment, and system-level specification (deciding exactly how the whole product should work).

Designers, product managers, engineers, and marketers now create more of their work inside Figma. So do the frontier AI labs themselves. In Q2, revenue growth accelerated for a third straight quarter, to 48%. And in the first full quarter of charging for AI, gross profit growth sped up too.

  • Q2 revenue grew 48% to $370 million. That is the third straight quarter of faster growth
  • Gross margin rose 2.5 points from Q1, to 85%. Gross profit grew 40% from a year earlier, to $314 million
  • About two-thirds of paid customers spending more than $10,000 a year added full seats at renewal, in line with prior quarters. Heavy AI use did not cut into seats: by the end of Q2, over 80% of all $10,000-plus customers used AI credits weekly
  • One of the world’s largest technology companies bought an AI credit add-on covering more than 25,000 paid seats. Engineers there now hold more paid seats than designers
  • Figma raised its full-year revenue outlook by $40 million, to about $1.465 billion, or 39% growth. It expects Q3 growth to slow to 36%, partly because last year’s price changes no longer boost the comparison with a year earlier

Underneath the headline, AI revenue finally caught up with AI cost. Credits were billed for the full quarter, and gross margin rose from Q1 instead of falling. This means AI usage on Figma now pays its own way. Paid usage lifts profit instead of diluting it, which is why gross profit growth sped up nine points in a single quarter. But the next wave of usage is still free. The Figma agent, Code Layers, Motion, and generative plugins run in beta, and their credit use is not billed. Already, by July 31, over half of paid customers spending more than $10,000 a year used the agent weekly. More than 20% of weekly credit users on paid plans used credits only through it. So the agent brings new people to AI on Figma, not just more prompts from the same ones. None of this sits in the revenue outlook. The 36% Q3 figure counts only the meters already running.

The bottom line

As AI cheapens the production of software, the scarce work moves from writing code to deciding what to build. Figma is the platform where that scarce work happens at scale. We own a piece of every dollar that flows through it.

Salesforce CRM

MegatrendCompanies are turning their customer relationships into a workforce of AI agents. Those agents sell, serve, and market on top of the company’s own Salesforce data.

Tens of thousands of businesses are turning their Salesforce systems into agentic enterprises (companies run partly by AI agents). More and more AI work now reaches Salesforce through other companies’ tools, including Anthropic’s Claude. In Q2, revenue rose 11% to $11.35 billion, above the top of the company’s own forecast. The quarter also tested two fears about what AI does to Salesforce. Customers were expected to pay Salesforce less; instead, bookings of Agentforce editions more than doubled from Q1. Contracts were expected to shrink; instead, they got longer in every customer segment.

  • cRPO (contracted revenue due over the next twelve months) reached $33.5 billion, up 14%, one point ahead of plan. Free cash flow rose 81% to $1.1 billion.
  • Agentforce (Salesforce’s AI-agent product) reached $1.5 billion in annual recurring revenue (ARR, the yearly value of its subscriptions). Total AI and data ARR is close to $4 billion. Agentforce bookings doubled from a year ago. Half of those bookings came from customers who used up their credits and bought more.
  • Customers ran 3.2 billion agentic work units (individual tasks the agents completed) in Q2 alone, up 97% from the prior quarter. Accounts with agents in live use grew 70% in the quarter, as 2,000 more paying customers went live.
  • The U.S. Army signed a multibillion-dollar expansion. Its Human Resources Command expects up to 55 million Agentforce conversations a month.
  • Slack, which Salesforce owns, grew new sales at its fastest pace since the acquisition. Active users of Slackbot, its built-in AI assistant, rose 150% in the quarter to 1 million, five months after launch. Nine of the top ten AI companies use Salesforce and Slack; their spending with Salesforce rose 435% in a year.

Underneath the headline, the AI tools that were supposed to replace Salesforce are sending it more work. Use of its apps by outside AI agents rose sixfold. Seats in sales, service, and Slack all grew year over year. Attrition is near its lowest level ever. This means Claude, ChatGPT, and custom-built apps are becoming new front doors to Salesforce, not replacements for it. Each request still runs on the customer’s own records, permissions, and rules, and those live inside Salesforce. The company charges for that access. Claudeforce, the new Claude-on-Salesforce product, and Headless (opening the platform to outside tools) both require premium editions. Those cost 60% to 80% more than standard ones. Only 5% of sales and service users have upgraded. So every new front door opens onto revenue Salesforce has barely begun to collect.

The bottom line

Salesforce is the system of record (the master copy of the data) where the world’s companies already keep their customers. It is becoming the place where AI agents go to work on that data. The agents sell, serve, and market on the company’s behalf. The customer data already lives there. The agents run where the data lives. Every new agent a customer switches on deepens a relationship that is already hard to leave. That is the kind of business we want to own.

HubSpot HUBS

MegatrendSmall and mid-sized businesses now get enterprise-grade AI agents inside the software they already use. That software runs their marketing, sales, and service. So a small team can operate like a big one.

More small and mid-sized businesses run their whole go-to-market (marketing, sales, and service) on HubSpot. Now they turn its Breeze AI agents loose to do the work. Q2 was a stumble, partly by choice. In April, HubSpot let customers try agents on their own data before buying, cut agent prices, and let customers cap their AI spend. Cautious buyers also pushed big purchases up to CEOs and boards. So new customers fell short of plan, and HubSpot cut its outlook for the rest of the year. Yet AI usage grew through the price cuts, revenue still rose 17% at constant currency (ignoring exchange-rate swings), and margins widened.

  • Revenue grew 20% year over year in Q2, or 17% at constant currency. Operating margin rose 3 points to 20%. Slower hiring drove the gain
  • HubSpot added 7,000 net new customers in Q2, short of its own plan of 9,000 to 10,000. It now expects 5,000 to 6,000 a quarter for the rest of the year
  • Last year’s customers now spend 2% more than they did a year ago (net revenue retention of 102%), down from 3% more. Their added seats and AI credits kept lifting spend, but more of them trimmed their plans at renewal to save money. The base held steady underneath: before any upgrades, HubSpot kept revenue in the high 80s percent of what those customers paid last year
  • Agent adoption kept climbing, and the agents do real work. Data Agent has over 16,000 customers, up 80% in the quarter. Prospecting Agent has almost 17,000, up 28%. Customer Agent (the AI support rep) now closes 72% of tickets with no human involved
  • The company cut its outlook. It expects Q3 revenue up 15% at constant currency and full-year growth of 16%. But it held its full-year operating profit target

Underneath the stumble, AI is changing how small businesses buy software. They now want proof in their own data, and a predictable bill, before they commit. HubSpot rebuilt its sale around both and accepted slower deals as the price. Large deals still close, just weeks later than before. The slowdown is in winning new revenue, not keeping it. Customers who already pay mostly stay, and they use the agents more. HubSpot now charges fewer credits (the metered fuel that runs the agents) for each agent task, and customers can cap their spend. On their own, both changes would shrink total credit use. Yet credit use still grew in Q2, because customers gave the agents much more work. Monthly agent actions tripled this year. But part of that work runs on free trials, which earn nothing until the customer buys. Revenue follows when trials turn into purchases: after proving an agent in a trial, one customer bought more than 1 million credits.

The bottom line

Owning HubSpot means we own the customer platform that small and mid-sized businesses run their marketing, sales, and service on. Increasingly, we also own the AI agents that do that work for them. We earn more each time a customer adds a hub, adds a seat, or lets an agent resolve more tickets and source more leads. All of it sits on top of growth context no rival can copy. Every small company wants to operate like a big one. HubSpot is building the platform that lets them.

Cluster III  ·  4 Businesses

Rebuilding Discovery, Decision, and Purchase

Four businesses rebuilding the layer between people and the things they buy. The activity is rewire how billions of consumers find, evaluate, and transact, a layer being rewritten in real time as AI becomes the new intermediary between intent and inventory.

Shopify SHOP

MegatrendShopping is moving into AI chat windows. The rails underneath must be rebuilt to handle it.

Merchants of every size run more commerce through Shopify, from new entrepreneurs to General Motors and L’Oréal. AI shopping assistants like ChatGPT and Gemini do the same. Q2 showed how those assistants fit in: they are one more front door into the checkout Shopify already runs. Through that door, buyers with specific needs reach small, specialized shops, which make up most of Shopify’s merchants. Each of those sales earns Shopify the same fee as a sale on the merchant’s own store. AI orders are still small next to total merchant sales, so Q2’s growth came from the core business. Merchant sales rose 32% to $116 billion, making Q2 the fifth straight quarter of growth above 30%.

  • AI assistants send buyers to niche products. In Q2, 75% of AI orders came from outside Shopify’s top 100 categories. Search ranks products by popularity. An agent matches several needs at once, like a car seat that fits three across a sedan, so the specialized product wins.
  • Clean product data wins those matches. AI searches using Shopify’s Catalog (the product data AI assistants read to find items) converted twice as often as searches on scraped data. Catalog products arrive complete and accurate, so an agent can match them to the buyer’s need.
  • AI shopping adds to search instead of replacing it. AI-driven traffic and orders to Shopify stores each tripled from a year ago. Traditional search sessions also kept growing, to 1.3 times their level two years earlier.
  • Buyers from AI assistants arrive ready to buy. Half of AI-referred visits land directly on a product page, which is 2.5 times the rate from traditional search. The buyer skips the browsing because the comparison already happened inside the assistant.
  • Every Shopify merchant is ready for UCP (the open standard Shopify co-built so agents can shop for buyers). Agents can build carts and check out through UCP, but every order still flows through Shopify. So each merchant’s checkout and fulfillment rules hold on any AI surface.

The new door feeds a merchant base that already compounds. Shopify’s growth rests on merchants who stay and keep growing. Merchants who joined in Q1 2015 now sell 5 times as much each quarter as when they started. Over five years, 92% of merchants who reached $1 million in yearly sales stayed on Shopify. AI shoppers favor small, specialized shops, which is where that climb begins. So each AI sale adds to a base that keeps growing for years.

The bottom line

Shopify benefits whether commerce flows through traditional storefronts or AI shopping assistants. The rails are the same, and we own them. As more channels open up, more transactions flow through Shopify’s payment infrastructure. We earn on every one.

Pinterest PINS

MegatrendVisual, AI-assisted shopping is replacing keyword search as the way people decide what to buy.

Pinterest’s 640 million users now ask the platform more commercial questions. Advertisers trust Pinterest’s AI to do more of their targeting. Q2 showed those questions starting to turn into ad revenue. Revenue growth in the U.S. and Canada rose to 18% from 13% in Q1, while users there grew only 4%. So nearly all of the gain came from earning more per existing user. Better AI bidding and a rebuilt sales team turned those users’ shopping interest into advertiser spend. Europe slowed for two reasons. New regulations cut spending by Asia-based retailers selling across borders, and a reorganized international sales team is still finding its footing. But that reorganization is how the U.S. playbook reaches Europe, so Europe’s slowdown comes partly from installing the same fix.

  • Each user in the U.S. and Canada is doing more on Pinterest. In Q2, searches and boards created there grew faster than users. So Pinterest’s most mature market still adds shopping interest to sell, even as its user count barely grows.
  • For several quarters, growth leaned on cheap ad views from newer international markets, which held average ad prices down. In Q2 that mix swung back. Ad prices rose 1% because more ad views came from the U.S. and Canada, where demand is stronger and ads cost more.
  • A handful of Pinterest’s largest advertisers now connect their own measurement systems to Pinterest’s AI bidding. The bidding then aims at each advertiser’s own goal, such as customer lifetime value (a customer’s total spending over years), rather than the click. The pilot contributed to Q2’s gain and widens to a few more advertisers in Q3.
  • Performance+, Pinterest’s AI ad-automation suite, does the campaign work that smaller advertisers lack the staff to do. In testing, new ad-delivery models in Performance+ raised small advertisers’ return on ad spend (sales earned per ad dollar) by 28%. Early next year, a simpler setup will start nearly every lower-funnel campaign (ads meant to produce a purchase now) on automation by default.
  • Growth in the U.S. and Canada now reaches well beyond the largest retailers. Mid-sized and small advertisers grew strongly, as did newer categories like financial services, travel, and health. The quarter showed that Pinterest’s limit is getting paid for the shopping it already drives, not finding more users. The bottom line

Every save, click, and search on Pinterest builds a data set unique to Pinterest. As AI-assisted shopping displaces keyword search, ad dollars flow toward the platforms that hold consumer-intent data. We own one of the most powerful.

The Trade Desk TTD

MegatrendThe open internet is now the largest single advertising market on earth. It spans connected TV, podcasts, news, sports, and retail media. It is split across thousands of publishers, and it grows every quarter.

From Bayer and IKEA to Nestle and Cheerios, the world’s largest brands trust Trade Desk’s AI to handle more of their ad-buying. They lock themselves in with multi-year contracts. The bottom line

Most of advertising’s biggest businesses (Google, Meta, Amazon) sell their own inventory. Trade Desk is the rare platform with no inventory of its own. Modernizing the Activities of Everyday Life

Three businesses replacing legacy ways of doing daily things (learning a language, ordering from a local merchant, running a restaurant) with modern, AI-enabled platforms. Duolingo DUOL

MegatrendAI tutors are about to make personalized learning available to a billion people for the price of a coffee.

Duolingo’s language learners run more AI-tutored conversations every day. In Q2, daily active users grew 23% from a year ago, faster than in Q1, because more learners kept coming back. But bookings (what customers commit to pay) grow more slowly, because new users take time to start paying. Duolingo has also paused any pricing move that slows user growth. At the same time, the cost of each AI tutoring call collapsed. So Duolingo can widen access to its AI tutor and still raise its margin target.

  • The share of current learners who keep coming back rose about 1 point in a year, to an all-time high. Duolingo got there by testing hundreds of product changes and keeping those that work. The gain shows up in every region and across all types of users.
  • In June, a one-time Streak Revival campaign let learners who had lost a long streak earn it back by finishing 3 lessons. More than 15 million did, and they are staying at higher rates than a typical group of returning users. A broken streak still holds value, so the record of past effort can pull lapsed learners back.
  • Duolingo moved most free trials from 7 days to one month. More people start the trial, and more of them go on to pay. Ads and energy limits (the cap on free practice) switch off during the trial, so trial users use the app more. The longer trial lifts paying users and daily users at once.
  • A video call with Duolingo’s AI tutor cost about 30 cents when the feature launched. It now costs under one cent, mainly because Duolingo moved to cheaper open-source models with no loss in quality that Duolingo can detect. A beginner who knows 100 words chats about frying eggs, not philosophy, so a model slightly behind the leaders works just as well.
  • AI cost per use is now falling faster than AI use is growing. So Duolingo now expects full-year gross margin near 70%, up from its initial 69% forecast, even as AI does more of the teaching. The same savings lifted the full-year adjusted EBITDA margin target to 26.5%, from the 25% set at the start of the year.

Underneath the quarter, cheaper AI now feeds the habit that drives Duolingo’s growth. Duolingo grows mainly by keeping learners. Retention compounds, so a small rise in the share who return becomes large user growth over time. Talking with the AI tutor feeds that habit, because learners who speak more learn better, stay engaged and spread the word. At launch, each call cost enough to confine video calls to Max, the most expensive plan. Now each call costs too little to ration, so most new Super subscribers already get video calls. Cost no longer decides who gets to practice speaking. Duolingo can now place the tutor in whichever plan best keeps learners and turns them into payers.

The bottom line

Tutoring used to mean a human teacher charging thousands of dollars. Meanwhile, hundreds of millions of people around the world pay to learn English to unlock economic opportunity. Duolingo brings tutoring to all of them, cheaper than a human teacher and more personalized than a classroom. We own the leading platform doing it at scale.

DoorDash DASH

  • The loop is speeding up. DoorDash added more DashPass members over the past year than in either of the two years before. In Q2, US paid-member growth was among the fastest in about two years
  • The US restaurant speed-up came from DoorDash’s own customers, not from one-off events. Much of it came from new DashPass members. Long-time customers also used DoorDash more than ever before
  • The loop runs abroad too. Wolt+, the DashPass equivalent at DoorDash’s Wolt unit, had a record quarter for paid-member growth. Deliveroo’s subscriber growth was its fastest in a couple of years
  • The profit beat had two sources. In the second half of Q2, ad income per order and average order size came in ahead of plan. Deliveroo’s order volume also beat DoorDash’s internal plan

As more customers join DashPass, gross profit per customer rises, so the loop that lifts orders also lifts profit.

The bottom line

Local commerce (restaurants, groceries, retail, pharmacy) sits about a decade behind digital media in moving onto on-demand platforms. DoorDash is the leading consolidator of that migration in the US, and now in Europe. We are owners of the operating system being built for it.

Three businesses capturing generational consumer-behavior shifts in food and drink as category winners. The activity is take share where the category is being rebuilt. Dutch Bros BROS

Vital Farms VITL

MegatrendConsumers pay more for food they trust. They want food that is humanely raised and transparently sourced, even in basic grocery categories like eggs and butter.

16 million U.S. households now reach for Vital Farms in the supermarket cold case. Q2 was the bill for a deliberate choice. When conventional egg prices collapsed this year, Vital Farms held its own price rather than cut right away. The gap to cheaper eggs widened, fewer shoppers tried the brand for the first time, and eggs piled up. Selling that surplus for pennies drove a 10.1% revenue drop and a $26.6 million adjusted EBITDA loss. But the surplus measured demand at Vital Farms’ old price, not demand for the brand. At one large retailer where Vital Farms narrowed its gap, sales per store and new households both rose 27%.

  • Vital Farms’ average shelf price slipped only from about $8.75 to $8.16, while mainstream store-brand eggs fell 52%. That widened the gap a first-time buyer had to cross. Earlier this year, first-time households fell to 50% of buyers, from more than 55% in 2024 and 2025.
  • Regular buyers did not leave. Households that already bought Vital Farms bought 2% more than their prior average, and the brand gained more than 2 points of egg-category dollar share. By mid-July, units sold per item per store per week were up 12.5% from May.
  • Now Vital Farms is closing the gap on purpose. Its average premium over branded rivals fell from about $2.51 in Q1 to $2.36 in Q2, on the way to a $1 to $2 target.
  • The surplus is being cut at the source. Amended farmer contracts now reduce supply instead of sending extra eggs to breaker plants, which crack them for liquid egg products and pay pennies a dozen. Q2 was the peak, at more than $20 million of lost profit.
  • The same supply build that caused the glut is filling new shelf space. Distribution points (a measure of how widely a product is stocked) should reach 170 to 175 in Q4, up from an average of 130 in 2025, and that shelf space is already sold in. It is why Vital Farms held its full-year sales guidance of $775 million to $800 million.

Underneath the loss, Vital Farms is trading margin for reach. Its premium was set when egg prices were high, and holding it after they fell priced out first-time buyers. Those buyers matter most, because Vital Farms grows by winning trial and then keeping the household. A smaller gap brings them back, and each one still pays a premium, on shelf space already won. The cost is a lower margin ceiling: gross margin should recover to about 30% by Q4, below the mid-30s of past years. Pooled buying on about $125 million of yearly feed helps pay for the smaller premium. So Vital Farms is giving up a few points of margin to rebuild a larger base of buyers, and that base is what earns the money over time.

The bottom line

The durable picture is unchanged. Demand for pasture-raised eggs outruns a supply that can only grow as fast as new hens and farms. Consumers pay a real premium for those eggs. This year is the temporary exception: a price shock briefly let supply run ahead. So Vital Farms is right-sizing production and closing the price gap rather than chasing volume. As the pull returns, the path is the one it always was. Bring more of those eggs to market, and we earn alongside every carton.

Celsius Holdings CELH

MegatrendSugar-free functional energy drinks are taking everyday market share from old-line sodas and traditional energy brands. The shift is strongest among women and fitness-oriented consumers.

Celsius now sells three energy-drink brands: Celsius, Alani Nu, and Rockstar. Together they hold ~20% of the U.S. energy-drink market. Q2 tested that mix. Revenue rose 11% to $818 million, but the original Celsius brand shrank and profit fell.

  • Q2 net revenue rose about 11% year over year to $818 million, including about $66 million from Rockstar
  • Alani Nu net sales reached about $364 million, up ~21%, while scanner sales (what shoppers actually bought at the register) grew ~56%
  • The original Celsius brand’s net sales fell about 12%, while scanner sales fell only 2%. Distributors drawing down their stock at quarter-end explained about half the gap. The company expects Q3 to look like Q2 for the brand, with growth returning as the year ends
  • Adjusted EBITDA fell to $184 million from $210 million a year ago. Its margin fell to 22.5% of revenue, from roughly 28% a year earlier. Gross margin was near 48%, the same as Q1 but lower than a year ago, as aluminum costs rose
  • Selling and overhead costs (SG&A) stayed flat at $238 million while revenue grew, so they fell to 29% of revenue from 32%, an efficiency gain

The bottom line

Celsius has assembled three advantages at once: a multi-brand portfolio, the largest beverage distribution network in the country (through PepsiCo), and authority over how the category’s shelf is organized. The combination makes the position durable, not any one piece.
